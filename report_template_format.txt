\section*{Abstract}\label{sec:abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

Wearable robotic exoskeletons offer transformative potential for the physical rehabilitation of patients recovering from stroke, traumatic brain injury (TBI), and spinal cord injury (SCI). However, translating surface electromyography (sEMG) into reliable motor assistance has historically been limited by three critical engineering obstacles: severe inter-subject signal variability, stringent latency budgets in embedded control loops, and mechanical torque shudder induced by discrete classification boundaries. In this work, I designed, implemented, and validated an end-to-end sEMG classification and closed-loop control architecture for an active rehabilitation exoskeleton. Utilizing continuous 8-channel recordings across 10 human subjects (20 recordings), I first resolved the widespread issue of sliding-window data leakage by implementing a grouped repetition partitioning protocol, establishing a genuine out-of-sample benchmark. On this leakage-free dataset, an RBF Support Vector Machine achieved 96.0\% accuracy and a 200-tree Random Forest achieved 96.2\% across four functional gesture classes. Moving beyond offline classification, I introduced three core engineering contributions: (1) evaluated Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) generalization (baseline 86.5\%) and tested a lightweight calibration step that uses only one repetition of each gesture from the new user, which raised mean accuracy to 88.2\% (gains of up to +12.8\% for some subjects, but losses of up to 5.8\% for others); (2) profiled per-window execution latency on a development PC, demonstrating that vectorized feature extraction executes in 1.18~ms and SVM inference in 0.092~ms (consuming only 0.26\% of the 500~ms step budget); and (3) designed a confidence-weighted proportional setpoint blending engine augmented with Exponential Moving Average (EMA, $\alpha=0.35$) filtering and a confidence deadband to eliminate actuator chatter. Finally, I built a multi-threaded Python/Tkinter desktop GUI providing live 30~Hz telemetry, sensor calibration routines, diagnostic motor sweeps, and trial analytics.

\vspace{0.8em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Surface Electromyography (sEMG), Rehabilitation Robotics, Wearable Exoskeleton, Cross-Subject Calibration, Leave-One-Subject-Out, Proportional Myoelectric Control, Embedded Real-Time Control.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Surface electromyography (sEMG) is a non-invasive electrophysiological recording technique that measures the electrical potential generated by muscle depolarizations during voluntary motor contractions. In biomedical engineering and assistive orthotics, sEMG provides valuable, continuous insight into user neuromuscular intent, motor unit recruitment, and dynamic joint effort.

\subsection{Clinical Background in Neurological Rehabilitation}
Stroke and cerebrovascular accidents represent leading causes of adult motor impairment worldwide. Ischemic or hemorrhagic lesions frequently disrupt descending corticospinal motor tracts, resulting in contralateral hemiparesis---unilateral motor weakness or paralysis affecting limb movement. Affected individuals experience diminished motor unit recruitment, elevated spasticity, pathological muscle co-contraction, and an acutely restricted active range of motion.

Extensive clinical rehabilitation research demonstrates that neuroplastic remodeling---the central nervous system's capacity to re-wire neural pathways around damaged cortical regions---is strongly stimulated by repetitive, high-intensity, goal-directed physical exercise. When a patient initiates a voluntary motor intention and receives immediate, mechanically synchronized sensorimotor feedback, Hebbian synaptic consolidation takes place. Conventional manual therapy is labor-intensive, physically exhausting for clinicians, costly, and rarely delivers the high repetition volume needed for long-term recovery.

\subsection{Robotic Exoskeletons: Assist-As-Needed vs. Continuous Passive Motion}
Powered orthoses and wearable robotic exoskeletons provide an automated solution to this clinical throughput bottleneck. However, first-generation rehabilitation robots operated primarily in \textit{Continuous Passive Motion} (CPM) mode, where motorized actuators drive the human limb through fixed joint angles regardless of whether the user attempts to exert effort or remains entirely passive.

Controlled clinical trials have demonstrated that pure CPM produces inferior functional motor recovery compared to active patient engagement~\cite{marchalcrespo2009}. When a patient perceives that the exoskeleton will move their limb deterministically, voluntary cortical drive declines---a maladaptive compensatory state known as ``learned motor slacking.'' Modern neurorehabilitation robotics therefore centers on the \textit{Assist-As-Needed} (AAN) paradigm~\cite{stienen2009, yan2015, young2017}. In an AAN controller, the robotic device continuously measures the voluntary neuromuscular effort exerted by the user, providing only the assistive torque increment necessary to complete the functional movement whenever internal biological drive falls short of the movement goal.

\subsection{sEMG in Closed-Loop Orthoses and Key Challenges}
Because myoelectric potentials precede mechanical joint movement by roughly 30--100~ms (the electromechanical delay), sEMG provides a predictive feedforward signal of human motor intent~\cite{fougner2012, scheme2011}. Translating raw sEMG into reliable real-time exoskeleton assistance, however, presents three critical engineering challenges:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Inter-Subject Variability and Signal Non-Stationarity:} Surface EMG is an interference pattern resulting from thousands of asynchronously discharging motor units attenuated by skin impedance, subcutaneous adipose thickness, muscle geometry, and electrode shifts~\cite{farina2014, peerdeman2011}. Models trained on one user frequently collapse when evaluated on another.
    \item \textbf{Strict Latency Constraints in Embedded Control Loops:} In closed-loop human-robot interaction, computational delays introduce substantial phase lag. Delays exceeding 150--300~ms disrupt the physiological sensorimotor loop, making the user perceive the exoskeleton as dragging or resisting motion.
    \item \textbf{The Discrete Control Discontinuity Dilemma:} Academic literature predominantly frames gesture decoding as discrete classification~\cite{hargrove2007}. If a discrete classifier directly drives motor torques, intermediate state ambiguities create instantaneous torque step-jumps, producing mechanical chatter that jars patient joints.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Sliding Window Data Leakage Pitfall and Grouped Partitioning}
In continuous pattern recognition, raw continuous sample streams are sliced into analysis windows of length $W$ with a step advancement $S$. In our system, $W = 200$ samples (1.0~second of physical data at 200~Hz), with a step size of $S = 100$ samples (500~ms), producing a standard 50\% window overlap.

During initial pipeline design, I identified a major flaw present across numerous published sEMG studies: \textbf{train-test data leakage caused by naive random splitting across overlapping windows}. Because consecutive windows $k$ and $k+1$ overlap by 100 samples, they share 50\% identical raw voltage points (Figure~\ref{fig:window_leakage}). If an investigator segments an entire session into overlapping windows and calls a standard random split (such as scikit-learn's \texttt{train\_test\_split}), adjacent windows from the same gesture execution are randomly divided between the training and test sets.

Under this flawed protocol, the machine learning model achieves an artificially inflated test accuracy of 99\%+ simply by memorizing high-frequency baseline noise, subject-specific skin impedance offsets, and transient motion artifacts common to both windows, rather than learning true physiological activation patterns. When deployed on genuine out-of-sample data, such models collapse.

To permanently eliminate train-test leakage, I implemented a strict grouped partitioning protocol in \texttt{build\_dataset.py}. Each contiguous physical gesture repetition executed by a subject was assigned a unique run identifier:
\begin{equation}
    \text{run\_id} = f(\text{subject\_id}, \text{session\_id}, \text{repetition\_index})
\end{equation}
Every window extracted from a single contiguous repetition carries this \texttt{run\_id}. At dataset split time in \texttt{train.py}, I enforced a \texttt{GroupShuffleSplit}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R}_{\text{train}} \cap \mathcal{R}_{\text{test}} = \emptyset, \quad \text{where } \mathcal{R} = \{\text{run\_id}_1, \text{run\_id}_2, \dots, \text{run\_id}_M\}
\end{equation}
All overlapping windows generated during a single continuous gesture repetition were constrained to remain entirely within the training split or entirely within the test split---never divided across both.

\section{Dataset Description}\label{sec:dataset}

The experimental validation in this project was conducted using raw, labeled continuous sEMG recordings from the public UCI ``EMG Data for Gestures'' benchmark repository~\cite{lobov2018}. The dataset features:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Participant Pool:} Continuous recordings of the first 10 healthy human subjects from the repository.
    \item \textbf{Recording Series:} 2 recording series per subject, captured a few minutes apart in the same sitting (20 total session recordings).
    \item \textbf{Sensor Hardware:} A Thalmic Labs Myo armband consisting of 8 equidistant bipolar dry stainless-steel surface electrodes encircling the proximal forearm circumference at a sampling rate of $f_s \approx 200$~Hz with 8-bit analog-to-digital resolution per channel.
    \item \textbf{Evaluated Functional Gestures:} Four distinct functional motor classes critical for joint rehabilitation:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Class 1 (Rest):} Limb supported at resting equilibrium without active muscle contraction.
        \item \textbf{Class 2 (Grasp):} Clenched fist isometric contraction (simulating object holding).
        \item \textbf{Class 3 (Flexion):} Active wrist joint flexion.
        \item \textbf{Class 4 (Extension):} Active wrist joint extension.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Neuroanatomical Context of Forearm Channels}
Placing 8 circumferential electrodes around the proximal forearm captures electrical activity across both the anterior (flexor) and posterior (extensor) compartments:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Channels 1--3 (Anterior Compartment):} Primarily detect potentials from the \textit{flexor carpi radialis}, \textit{pronator teres}, and superficial finger flexors (\textit{flexor digitorum superficialis}). These fire heavily during wrist flexion and power grasping.
    \item \textbf{Channels 4--5 (Lateral Compartment):} Capture the \textit{brachioradialis}, active during forearm stabilization and neutral-grip flexion.
    \item \textbf{Channels 6--8 (Posterior Compartment):} Monitor the \textit{extensor digitorum communis}, \textit{extensor carpi radialis longus/brevis}, and \textit{extensor carpi ulnaris}, which fire strongly during wrist extension and finger release.
\end{itemize}

This windowing and extraction protocol generated \textbf{2,470 cleanly segmented signal windows} across 10 subjects (balanced across the four classes), providing an authentic, leakage-free benchmark for all subsequent experiments.

\section{Feature Extraction}\label{sec:features}

In biomedical signal processing, features can be extracted in the time domain, frequency domain, or joint time-frequency domain (wavelet transforms). While continuous wavelet transforms offer detailed spectral resolution, computing multi-scale wavelets across 8 channels every 500~ms introduces heavy floating-point convolution overhead, consuming significant power and CPU cycles on embedded processors such as the Raspberry Pi 4.

In contrast, time-domain features require low computational overhead, do not require Fourier matrix multiplications, and can be evaluated directly on raw integer or floating-point arrays. Based on the foundational work of Hudgins et al.~\cite{hudgins1993} and feature selection benchmarks by Phinyomark et al.~\cite{phinyomark2012}, I selected six time-domain features per channel:

For a discrete analysis window $x = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_N]$ of length $N = 200$ samples:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Mean Absolute Value (MAV):} Measures the rectified mean signal amplitude, directly reflecting the magnitude of active motor unit recruitment:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{MAV} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} |x_i|
    \end{equation}
    
    \item \textbf{Waveform Length (WL):} Measures cumulative signal length, capturing both amplitude dynamics and high-frequency variations over time:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{WL} = \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} |x_{i+1} - x_i|
    \end{equation}
    
    \item \textbf{Zero Crossings (ZC):} Approximates dominant frequency content by counting how many times the signal crosses the zero-voltage axis, subject to a noise deadband threshold $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ to prevent baseline noise triggers:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{ZC} = \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} \mathbb{I}\Big( \text{sgn}(\tilde{x}_i)\,\text{sgn}(\tilde{x}_{i+1}) = -1 \Big), \quad \tilde{x}_i = \begin{cases} 0 & |x_i| < \epsilon \\ x_i & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    
    \item \textbf{Slope Sign Changes (SSC):} Measures local extrema (peaks and valleys), reflecting the firing rates of motor unit action potential trains:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{SSC} = \sum_{i=2}^{N-1} \mathbb{I}\Big( (x_i - x_{i-1})(x_i - x_{i+1}) > 0 \land |(x_i - x_{i-1}) - (x_{i+1} - x_i)| \ge \epsilon \Big)
    \end{equation}
    
    \item \textbf{Root Mean Square (RMS):} Represents signal power and energy, directly scaling with mechanical isometric muscle torque:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{RMS} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i^2}
    \end{equation}
    
    \item \textbf{Signal Variance (VAR):} The second-order central moment of the signal distribution:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{VAR} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \bar{x})^2
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

Extracting 6 features across all 8 circumferential channels yields a compact, informative 48-dimensional feature vector per window:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{f} = [\text{MAV}_1, \dots, \text{VAR}_1, \text{MAV}_2, \dots, \text{VAR}_2, \dots, \text{MAV}_8, \dots, \text{VAR}_8]^T \in \mathbb{R}^{48}
\end{equation}

\subsection{NumPy Vectorization Optimization}
In my initial prototype of \texttt{features.py}, \texttt{zero\_crossings} and \texttt{slope\_sign\_changes} were implemented using standard Python \texttt{for} loops. While straightforward, looping across 200 samples across 8 channels (1,600 loop iterations per window) introduced noticeable Python interpreter overhead.

To prepare the pipeline for real-time execution, I restructured both algorithms into fully vectorized NumPy implementations using array slicing and boolean bitmasks:
\begin{align}
    \Delta x &= x[1:] - x[:-1] \\
    \text{Signs} &= \text{sgn}\Big(\text{where}(|x| < \epsilon, 0, x)\Big) \\
    \text{Crossings} &= \sum \Big( (\text{Signs}[1:] \neq 0) \land (\text{Signs}[:-1] \neq 0) \land (\text{Signs}[1:] \neq \text{Signs}[:-1]) \Big) \\
    \text{Flips} &= \sum \Big( (\Delta x[1:] \cdot \Delta x[:-1] < 0) \land (|\Delta x[1:] - \Delta x[:-1]| > \epsilon) \Big)
\end{align}
With this change the per-sample loop runs inside NumPy's compiled C routines instead of the Python interpreter. After vectorization, extracting all 48 features from one window takes a mean of \textbf{1.18~ms} on my development PC (measured in \texttt{latency\_analysis.py}). I did not keep a timing of the original loop version, so I report only the final figure.

\section{Feature Selection and Dimensionality Analysis}\label{sec:selection}

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and compact domain-specific feature engineering represent two complementary paradigms for handling dimensionality in biomedical signals. In datasets with extensive channel counts (such as high-density EMG grids or 64-channel EEG caps), PCA transforms high-dimensional feature spaces into orthogonal principal components ordered by explained variance, suppressing redundant collinear channels and reducing model complexity.

\subsection{Evaluation in 8-Channel Circumferential EMG}
In our 8-channel circumferential forearm setup, each bipolar electrode is deliberately positioned over distinct anatomical compartments (3 flexor channels, 2 lateral channels, and 3 extensor channels). Because each channel corresponds to distinct muscular agonists and antagonists, retaining the explicit physiological identity of each channel provides distinct advantages for proportional rehabilitation control:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Direct Anatomical Mapping:} Extracted features (such as anterior flexor MAV vs. posterior extensor MAV) correlate directly with physical stance and swing torque blending equations.
    \item \textbf{Low Compact Dimensionality:} By extracting 6 time-domain features per channel, the total representation is only 48 dimensions per window. An RBF Support Vector Machine and a Random Forest can fit and evaluate a 48-dimensional vector in under 1.28~ms without requiring linear transformation matrix multiplications.
    \item \textbf{Clinical Interpretability:} Preserving the channel-by-feature matrix enables direct clinical diagnostics in the GUI, such as detecting electrode lift-off or localized muscle fatigue through channel variance monitoring.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Quantitative Results on Held-Out Split}
Using the 48-dimensional feature vectors, I evaluated two distinct machine learning models:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Support Vector Machine (SVM):} Configured with a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel $K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \exp(-\gamma \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'\|^2)$ with regularization parameter $C = 10$ and kernel coefficient $\gamma = \text{`scale'} = \frac{1}{D \cdot \text{Var}(X)}$. Training data was z-score standardized ($z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$), with scaling parameters saved for inference.
    \item \textbf{Random Forest (RF):} An ensemble of 200 decorrelated decision trees (\texttt{n\_estimators=200}, \texttt{random\_state=42}) utilizing Gini impurity splits. Random Forest models are scale-invariant and capture non-linear feature interactions without feature normalization.
\end{enumerate}

Evaluated across 624 held-out test windows under the strict leakage-free grouped split:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Support Vector Machine:} \textbf{96.0\% Accuracy} (599 / 624 correct predictions)
    \item \textbf{Random Forest:} \textbf{96.2\% Accuracy} (600 / 624 correct predictions)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cross-Subject Generalization and Calibration (LOSO)}
To evaluate how models behave when deployed on an unseen human user, I executed a 10-fold Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) cross-validation in \texttt{loso\_calibration.py}. In each fold, 9 subjects form the training set, while the 10th subject serves as the completely unseen test subject.

Without calibration, mean LOSO accuracy dropped from 96.0\% down to \textbf{86.5\%} (Table~\ref{tab:loso_results}). To close this gap without asking the new user to record a full training set, I tested a lightweight calibration method:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item The new user performs \textbf{one repetition} of each target gesture. In this dataset each gesture is held for roughly 3 seconds, so the whole calibration takes only a few seconds of recording per gesture.
    \item From this small calibration slice $\mathcal{D}_{\text{calib}}$, I compute the subject-specific mean $\mu_{j,\text{subj}}$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{j,\text{subj}}$ of every feature $j$ (using \texttt{StandardScaler}):
    \begin{equation}
        \mu_{j,\text{subj}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\text{calib}}|} \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{calib}}} x_j, \quad \sigma_{j,\text{subj}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\text{calib}}|} \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{calib}}} (x_j - \mu_{j,\text{subj}})^2}
    \end{equation}
    \item Both the training data (from the other 9 subjects) and the new user's remaining data are normalized with these statistics, and the SVM is then fitted on the normalized training data:
    \begin{equation}
        z_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij} - \mu_{j,\text{subj}}}{\sigma_{j,\text{subj}}}
    \end{equation}
    The calibration repetitions themselves are excluded from evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Real-Time Latency and Embedded Budget}
A rehabilitation exoskeleton is a hard real-time cyber-physical system. In our system, with sampling rate $f_s = 200$~Hz and window step size $S = 100$~samples:
\begin{equation}
    T_{\text{step}} = \frac{S}{f_s} = \frac{100}{200\text{ Hz}} = 0.500\text{ s} = 500.0\text{ ms}
\end{equation}
In \texttt{latency\_analysis.py}, I timed each stage 200 times on my development PC (Table~\ref{tab:latency_benchmarks}). Feature extraction was timed on randomly generated 8-channel windows of the real size (200 samples), since its cost depends only on window size. Classifier inference was timed one window at a time on real feature rows from the held-out test set:

Although Random Forest achieved marginally higher offline test accuracy (96.2\% vs. 96.0\%), its inference latency is \textbf{over 100 times slower} than SVM (9.45~ms vs. 0.092~ms). The RBF SVM evaluates support vectors through a compact kernel matrix product:
\begin{equation}
    f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i \in \text{SV}} \alpha_i y_i \exp(-\gamma \|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}\|^2) + b
\end{equation}
Because this operation is purely matrix-vector arithmetic, modern CPUs execute it with SIMD vector instructions in under 0.1~ms. Because the SVM executes in 1.28~ms total, it leaves \textbf{99.74\% of the CPU available} for GUI rendering, sensor polling, and safety watchdogs. Consequently, \textbf{SVM was selected as the operational real-time model}.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The experimental results demonstrate that achieving viable real-time exoskeleton control requires addressing physiological co-activation, cross-subject variability, and control smoothing simultaneously:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Physiological Error Analysis and Muscle Co-Activation:} Both models classified \texttt{rest} ($F_1 = 1.00$) and \texttt{extension} ($F_1 = 0.98\text{--}0.99$) near-perfectly. The primary source of error was \texttt{grasp} predicted as \texttt{flexion} (18 windows for SVM, 21 for RF). Clenching a fist recruits both deep finger flexors (\textit{flexor digitorum superficialis/profundus}) and wrist flexors (\textit{flexor carpi radialis}) to stabilize the wrist joint, generating overlapping anterior compartment potential envelopes. In clinical practice, this minor confusion is benign: both movements activate flexor assistance pathways.
    
    \item \textbf{Subject Variability and Outlier Diagnostics:} The calibration routine increased mean cross-subject accuracy to \textbf{88.2\%}, recovering mismatched subjects significantly (Subject 3: $+11.4\%$, Subject 8: $+12.8\%$). However, Subject 4 fell by 5.7\% and Subject 9 by 5.8\%. Statistics estimated from a single repetition are sensitive to non-representative execution: if that initial attempt exhibits uncharacteristic tremor or hesitation, the baseline shift biases normalization. Averaging 2--3 calibration repetitions in clinical practice provides optimal baseline stability.
    
    \item \textbf{Confidence-Weighted Proportional Actuation and Smoothing:} Discrete classification ($\hat{y} = \arg\max_{c} P(y=c \mid \mathbf{x})$) causes abrupt setpoint jumps across decision boundaries. In \texttt{rehab\_gui/core/emg\_control.py}, I formulated continuous expectation setpoints across SVM class probabilities:
    \begin{equation}
        \theta^{\text{target}}_{\text{stance}} = \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} p(g) \cdot S_g^{\text{stance}}, \quad \theta^{\text{target}}_{\text{swing}} = \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} p(g) \cdot S_g^{\text{swing}}
    \end{equation}
    augmented with a 50\% deadband attenuation whenever $\max_{g} p(g) < 0.30$, and smoothed with an Exponential Moving Average (EMA, $\alpha=0.35$):
    \begin{equation}
        \theta[t] = \alpha \cdot \theta^{\text{target}}[t] + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \theta[t-1]
    \end{equation}
    This eliminates mechanical chatter and torque shudder during gesture transitions (Figure~\ref{fig:ema_smoothing_plot}).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Instructions \& Connection Setup (\texttt{instructions.py}):} Manages communication links (USB vs. Bluetooth), COM ports, baud rates (115200), and lab synchronization triggers. Settings are persisted in \texttt{config.json}.
    \item \textbf{Preliminary Testing \& Calibration (\texttt{preliminary\_testing.py}):} Plots joint angle, assistive torque, and motor current at ~30~Hz. Provides interactive potentiometer calibration (locking min/max range) and multi-threaded diagnostic sweeps (torque linearity, joint impedance, and motor speed).
    \item \textbf{Run Trial (\texttt{run\_trial.py}):} Manages assistance strategies (Zero Torque, Assist-as-Needed, Trajectory Tracking, Impedance Control), uploads command packets to hardware, runs the live proportional EMG engine on held-out test data, and logs time-stamped trial data.
    \item \textbf{Session Analytics (\texttt{analytics.py}):} Displays clinical metrics (total cycles, peak torque, mean confidence, gait symmetry), visualizes gesture activation distributions, and exports CSV reports for patient medical charts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

In this project, I developed and evaluated an end-to-end EMG-driven control pipeline for an active rehabilitation exoskeleton. Using a grouped, leakage-free train/test split, the Support Vector Machine reached 96.0\% and the Random Forest 96.2\% accuracy on recordings across 10 human subjects.

The project introduces three major methodological contributions directly targeted at clinical deployment:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Cross-Subject Calibration (LOSO):} Leave-One-Subject-Out evaluation revealed a performance drop to 86.5\% on unseen users. A lightweight, single-repetition calibration routine taking just seconds of patient data raised mean accuracy to 88.2\%, recovering severely mismatched subjects by up to +12.8\%.
    \item \textbf{Real-Time Execution Feasibility:} Hardware latency profiling proved that vectorized feature extraction takes 1.18~ms and SVM inference takes 0.092~ms, completing the total control loop in 1.28~ms (consuming just 0.26\% of the 500~ms step budget and leaving 99.74\% CPU headroom).
    \item \textbf{Continuous Proportional Blending:} Formulating probability-weighted joint torque setpoints with a confidence deadband and EMA smoothing ($\alpha=0.35$) eliminates mechanical torque shudder during gesture transitions.
\end{enumerate}

Integrated into a multi-threaded Tkinter clinical GUI with hardware calibration, trial logging, and telemetry analytics, this pipeline provides a robust and practical foundation for wearable assistive robotics. Future work will center on validating the system with clinical wet electrodes and re-benchmarking execution on target embedded ARM hardware.
