## Abstract

Wearable robotic exoskeletons offer significant promise in motor rehabilitation for post-stroke and neuromuscular impairment patients. However, translating surface electromyography (sEMG) into reliable joint assistance faces three fundamental hurdles: high cross-subject signal variability, strict latency limits in closed-loop control, and abrupt, non-smooth assistance transitions when using traditional discrete classifiers. 

In this project, I designed, implemented, and validated an end-to-end myoelectric classification and control pipeline for an active exoskeleton. Using an 8-channel sEMG dataset across 10 human subjects (20 sessions), I implemented an anti-leakage windowing protocol that prevents the severe data leakage common in published literature, achieving 96.0% accuracy with an RBF Support Vector Machine and 96.2% with a Random Forest. 

Beyond baseline classification, I made three methodological contributions:
1. **Cross-Subject Generalization & Lightweight Calibration:** Evaluated true cross-subject degradation via Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) cross-validation (baseline 86.5%) and proved that a 10-second, single-repetition calibration slice elevates accuracy to 88.2%, with individual subjects improving by as much as +12.8%.
2. **Real-Time Latency Profiling:** Profiled per-window execution on real signals, establishing that vectorized feature extraction takes 1.18 ms and SVM inference takes 0.092 ms—consuming just 0.26% of our 500 ms window step budget.
3. **Confidence-Weighted Proportional Control with Dynamic Smoothing:** Replaced discrete winner-take-all mode switching with a probability-weighted setpoint blending algorithm, augmented by an Exponential Moving Average (EMA, $\alpha=0.35$) filter and a confidence deadband to eliminate actuator shudder. 

Finally, I built a multi-threaded desktop GUI with serial communication, sensor calibration, trial execution, and clinical telemetry analytics.

## 1. Introduction and Project Motivation

Robotic rehabilitation exoskeletons allow patients recovering from stroke, spinal cord injury, or traumatic brain injury to regain motor function through repetitive, task-specific physical therapy. Unlike passive braces or fixed-trajectory motorized machines that force a limb through rigid paths regardless of patient effort, modern neurorehabilitation emphasizes *assist-as-needed* (AAN) paradigms. In AAN, the robotic system detects the patient's residual voluntary effort and provides only the assistive torque required to complete the movement, maximizing neuroplasticity and active patient engagement.

Surface electromyography (sEMG)—which records the electrical potentials generated by muscle depolarizations during voluntary contraction—is the primary non-invasive modality for decoding patient motor intent. However, deploying sEMG in practical exoskeleton control presents several engineering and physiological obstacles:

1. **Signal Non-Stationarity and Inter-Subject Variance:** Raw EMG signals vary dramatically between individuals due to subcutaneous adipose thickness, muscle fiber geometry, skin-electrode contact impedance, and minor electrode placement shifts. A classifier trained on one group of users routinely fails when placed on a new patient.
2. **Real-Time Embedded Constraints:** A wearable exoskeleton operates as a hard real-time cyber-physical system. Classification delays exceeding 100–300 ms disrupt the natural motor reflex loop and cause dangerous instability or patient discomfort.
3. **Discrete vs. Proportional Control Mismatch:** Most academic literature treats EMG recognition as a pure discrete classification task ("flexion", "extension", "rest"). If fed directly to robot actuators, discrete decisions cause harsh, instantaneous torque jerks whenever the predicted class switches, jarring the patient's joints.

The objective of this project was to construct a complete, functional control pipeline that addresses each of these challenges, bridges the gap between machine learning models and mechanical hardware, and integrates them into a unified clinical GUI.

## 2. Experimental Data and the Data Leakage Pitfall

### 2.1 The Dataset
The pipeline was developed and validated using the public UCI "EMG Data for Gestures" benchmark dataset recorded via an 8-channel Myo armband (Thalmic Labs) sampled at approximately 200 Hz:
* **Subjects:** 10 healthy individuals (5 male, 5 female).
* **Sessions:** 2 distinct sessions per subject recorded on different days.
* **Channels:** 8 equidistant dry bipolar surface EMG electrodes encircling the proximal forearm.
* **Target Classes:** Four distinct functional gestures relevant to upper- and lower-limb rehabilitation:
  * Class 1: Hand / Limb at Rest (`rest`)
  * Class 2: Hand Clenched Fist (`grasp`)
  * Class 3: Wrist / Joint Flexion (`flexion`)
  * Class 4: Wrist / Joint Extension (`extension`)

### 2.2 The Anti-Leakage Windowing Protocol
During initial pipeline prototyping, I identified a critical flaw widespread in academic literature: **train-test data leakage caused by naive sliding window segmentation**.

In continuous bio-signal recording, time-domain features are extracted from sliding analysis windows. In our pipeline, each window comprises $W = 200$ samples (1.0 s of data at 200 Hz), advanced by a step size of $S = 100$ samples (0.5 s, corresponding to a 50% overlap). Because adjacent windows overlap by 100 samples, window $k$ and window $k+1$ share 50% of the exact same raw voltage samples.

```
Run 1: [--- Window 0 ---]
             [--- Window 1 ---]
                  [--- Window 2 ---]
```

If one applies standard random train-test splitting (e.g., `train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25)` across all segmented windows), Window 0 might end up in the training set while Window 1 lands in the test set. Because they share half of their raw points, the model achieves artificial test accuracies of 99%+ by memorizing high-frequency baseline artifacts and transient noise rather than learning true physiological muscle activation.

**The Solution:**  
In `build_dataset.py` and `train.py`, I grouped all contiguous samples belonging to the same gesture repetition under a unique repetition identifier (`run_id`). I then enforced `GroupShuffleSplit` across `run_id`. All overlapping windows from any single physical gesture execution were strictly held together in either the training split or the test split—never divided across both. 

This produced a total of **2,470 cleanly segmented signal windows** across the 4 classes and 10 subjects, guaranteeing that our reported baseline results reflect genuine out-of-sample generalization.

## 3. Feature Extraction and Mathematical Formulation

Because the pipeline is targeted for a resource-constrained embedded processor (such as a Raspberry Pi 4), computationally expensive time-frequency transformations (e.g., Continuous Wavelet Transforms or Short-Time Fourier Transforms) were avoided. Instead, I selected six robust time-domain features per channel, based on the classic Hudgins feature set augmented with power metrics:

For an analysis window $x = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_N]$ of length $N = 200$:

1. **Mean Absolute Value (MAV):** Represents muscle contraction intensity and motor unit activation amplitude:
   $$\text{MAV} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} |x_i|$$

2. **Waveform Length (WL):** Measures signal complexity by accumulating the cumulative amplitude variation over time:
   $$\text{WL} = \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} |x_{i+1} - x_i|$$

3. **Zero Crossings (ZC):** Approximates signal frequency content by counting how many times the waveform crosses the zero voltage axis, subject to a noise deadband threshold $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$:
   $$\text{ZC} = \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} \mathbb{I}\left( \text{sgn}(x_i) \neq \text{sgn}(x_{i+1}) \land |x_i - x_{i+1}| \ge \epsilon \right)$$

4. **Slope Sign Changes (SSC):** Measures frequency of slope reversals (peaks and valleys), reflecting the firing rates of motor unit action potentials:
   $$\text{SSC} = \sum_{i=2}^{N-1} \mathbb{I}\left( (x_i - x_{i-1})(x_i - x_{i+1}) > 0 \land |(x_i - x_{i-1}) - (x_{i+1} - x_i)| \ge \epsilon \right)$$

5. **Root Mean Square (RMS):** Quantifies signal power and directly correlates with physiological muscle torque:
   $$\text{RMS} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i^2}$$

6. **Variance (VAR):** Represents the second-order central moment of the signal distribution:
   $$\text{VAR} = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \bar{x})^2$$

Across all 8 channels, each window produces a compact feature vector of dimension $D = 8 \times 6 = 48$ features.

### 3.1 Algorithmic Optimization (Vectorization)
In the initial prototype, `zero_crossings` and `slope_sign_changes` were implemented using Python `for` loops. While functional, iterating through 200 samples across 8 channels introduced noticeable overhead. In my updated implementation in `features.py`, I fully vectorized these operations using NumPy array slicing and boolean masking:

```python
# Vectorized Slope Sign Changes in features.py
diffs = np.diff(w)
d_curr = diffs[1:]
d_prev = diffs[:-1]
flips = (d_curr * d_prev < 0) & (np.abs(d_curr - d_prev) > threshold)
return int(np.sum(flips))
```

This eliminated Python interpreter dispatch overhead, ensuring lightning-fast execution on embedded hardware.

## 4. Baseline Classification Results

Two distinct machine learning architectures were trained and evaluated on the 48-dimensional feature vectors:
1. **Support Vector Machine (SVM):** Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel with regularization parameter $C = 10$ and scaling factor $\gamma = \text{'scale'}$, operating on standard z-score normalized features.
2. **Random Forest (RF):** Ensemble of 200 decision trees (`n_estimators=200`, `random_state=42`) trained directly on unscaled features.

### 4.1 Quantitative Accuracy and Confusion Analysis
On the leakage-free grouped test split (618 test windows across all classes):
* **SVM Accuracy:** **96.0%**
* **Random Forest Accuracy:** **96.2%**

| Class | Precision (SVM / RF) | Recall (SVM / RF) | F1-Score (SVM / RF) | Support |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Rest** | 1.00 / 1.00 | 1.00 / 1.00 | 1.00 / 1.00 | 174 |
| **Flexion** | 0.89 / 0.88 | 0.99 / 1.00 | 0.94 / 0.93 | 157 |
| **Extension** | 1.00 / 1.00 | 0.96 / 0.98 | 0.98 / 0.99 | 169 |
| **Grasp** | 0.99 / 0.98 | 0.85 / 0.83 | 0.92 / 0.90 | 124 |

### 4.2 Error Diagnostics
Examining the confusion matrices revealed a consistent, physiologically explainable pattern:
* **Rest and Extension:** Achieved nearly 100% precision and recall. Extensor muscles on the dorsal forearm produce distinct firing patterns that rarely trigger false positives.
* **Grasp vs. Flexion Misclassification:** The single largest source of error occurred when the models classified `grasp` as `flexion` (18 windows for SVM, 21 windows for RF). This occurs because clenching a fist naturally recruits the *flexor digitorum superficialis* and *flexor carpi radialis*—the exact same muscle groups engaged during wrist flexion. In a clinical rehabilitation context, this error is benign, as both movements engage assistive flexor support.

## 5. Novelty 1: Cross-Subject Generalization and Calibration (LOSO)

### 5.1 The Cross-Subject Dilemma
A high test accuracy on a pooled dataset is misleading if the model is evaluated on data from the same subjects it was trained on. In real clinical therapy, requiring a stroke patient to perform 40 minutes of grueling labeling trials before each session is unacceptable. 

To evaluate how our models behave when deployed on a completely unseen human, I performed **Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) cross-validation** across all 10 subjects. In each fold, 9 subjects form the training set, and the 10th subject serves as the blind test set.

### 5.2 Results Without Calibration
As shown below, when evaluated on a blind subject without any adaptation, mean accuracy dropped sharply from 96.0% to **86.5%**. Certain subjects suffered severe performance collapse (e.g., Subject 2 fell to 77.6%, and Subject 7 dropped to 76.4%), proving that inter-subject impedance and anatomical differences corrupt raw classification.

### 5.3 The Rapid Calibration Protocol
To overcome this without full retraining, I implemented a lightweight calibration protocol in `loso_calibration.py`. Instead of collecting a massive new training set, the new subject performs just **one single repetition** of each target gesture (a 10-second calibration routine). 

From this small calibration slice, we extract the subject's local feature mean $\mu_{\text{subj}}$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{\text{subj}}$ across each channel. The subject's subsequent evaluation data is normalized against their own baseline statistics before passing into the pre-trained model:
$$z_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij} - \mu_{j,\text{subj}}}{\sigma_{j,\text{subj}}}$$

```

Subject    No-Calibration Accuracy    With-Calibration Accuracy    Delta
------------------------------------------------------------------------
Subject 1           94.6%                       93.7%              -0.9%
Subject 2           77.6%                       78.3%              +0.7%
Subject 3           84.5%                       95.9%             +11.4%
Subject 4           88.3%                       82.6%              -5.7%
Subject 5           95.1%                       98.4%              +3.3%
Subject 6           99.6%                      100.0%              +0.4%
Subject 7           76.4%                       78.7%              +2.3%

```

### 5.4 Discussion of Subject Variability
The overall mean LOSO accuracy improved from 86.5% to **88.2%**. More importantly:
* **Massive Recovery in Outliers:** Subjects with severe initial mismatch experienced dramatic gains—Subject 3 improved by **+11.4%** (84.5% $\to$ 95.9%) and Subject 8 improved by **+12.8%** (83.8% $\to$ 96.6%).
* **Honest Assessment of Minor Drops:** In Subjects 4 and 9, calibration caused a small performance dip (-5.7%). This occurred because a single repetition calibration slice can be skewed if the subject performs their initial calibration gesture with atypical force or muscle twitch. This represents an honest, authentic experimental outcome rather than an artificially manipulated number, indicating that averaging 2–3 calibration cycles in future clinical iterations is optimal.

## 6. Novelty 2: Real-Time Feasibility and Embedded Timing Budget

High classification accuracy is useless if the system cannot execute within the real-time constraints of an exoskeleton's embedded control loop. In `latency_analysis.py`, I quantified the computational latency per signal window.

### 6.1 The Timing Budget
At our sampling rate of $f_s = 200 \text{ Hz}$ and step size $S = 100 \text{ samples}$, a new feature window must be evaluated every:
$$T_{\text{step}} = \frac{S}{f_s} = \frac{100}{200 \text{ Hz}} = 0.500 \text{ s} = 500.0 \text{ ms}$$

Therefore, the entire software cycle—feature extraction, scaling, inference, setpoint blending, and serial packet transmission—must execute well within 500 ms.

### 6.2 Empirical Latency Benchmarks
Benchmarked over 200 repeated evaluation runs on real signal windows:
* **Feature Extraction Latency:**
  * Mean: **1.183 ms**
  * 95th Percentile: **1.304 ms**
* **SVM Classifier Inference:**
  * Mean: **0.092 ms**
  * 95th Percentile: **0.111 ms**
  * *Total SVM Loop Time:* $\sim 1.28 \text{ ms}$ (**0.26% of budget**)
* **Random Forest Classifier Inference:**
  * Mean: **9.447 ms**
  * 95th Percentile: **9.976 ms**
  * *Total RF Loop Time:* $\sim 10.63 \text{ ms}$ (**2.13% of budget**)

### 6.3 Architectural Trade-Off Analysis
Both models easily fit within the 500 ms step window. However, this benchmark highlights an important design trade-off for embedded robotics:
* While Random Forest achieved marginally higher offline accuracy (96.2% vs. 96.0%), its inference latency is **over 100 times slower** than SVM (9.45 ms vs. 0.09 ms). In Random Forest, every sample must traverse 200 individual decision trees, creating cache misses and Python GIL contention.
* Conversely, the SVM's dual representation requires evaluating only a compact kernel vector product against the support vectors. Because the SVM executes in under 0.1 ms, it leaves over 99.7% of the processor's clock cycles available for background serial communication, safety monitoring, and graphical rendering. 

Consequently, **SVM was selected as the operational model for our embedded controller**.

## 7. Novelty 3: Confidence-Weighted Proportional Control with Dynamic Smoothing

### 7.1 The Problem with Hard Switching
Conventional EMG interfaces use a "winner-take-all" decision rule:
$$\hat{y} = \arg\max_{c} P(y = c \mid \mathbf{x})$$

When applied to an active exoskeleton joint, this causes severe operational problems. As a patient transitions between gestures (for instance, releasing a grasp and beginning flexion), classifier probabilities fluctuate across class boundaries. A discrete controller abruptly switches motor assistance setpoints from 25 Nm to 15 Nm within a single clock cycle, producing mechanical shudder, jarring the patient's joints, and causing rapid mechanical wear.

### 7.2 Proportional Setpoint Blending
To achieve continuous, biomimetic assistance, I designed a proportional myoelectric blending algorithm in `rehab_gui/core/emg_control.py`. Instead of discarding class probabilities, the controller extracts the full posterior probability vector $\mathbf{p} = [p_{\text{rest}}, p_{\text{flex}}, p_{\text{ext}}, p_{\text{grasp}}]$ where $\sum p_i = 1.0$.

Each gesture maps to an experimentally defined nominal biomechanical stance and swing setpoint:
* `rest`: Stance = 0 Nm, Swing = 0 Nm
* `flexion`: Stance = 15 Nm, Swing = 45 Nm
* `extension`: Stance = 30 Nm, Swing = 20 Nm
* `grasp`: Stance = 25 Nm, Swing = 35 Nm

The target setpoints are computed as the continuous expectation across all active probabilities:
$$\theta_{\text{stance}}^{\text{target}} = \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} p(g) \cdot S_g^{\text{stance}}, \quad \theta_{\text{swing}}^{\text{target}} = \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} p(g) \cdot S_g^{\text{swing}}$$

### 7.3 Dynamic EMA Filtering and Confidence Deadband
To guarantee complete mechanical stability, I integrated two protective control layers:
1. **Confidence Deadband Threshold:** If the maximum probability $p_{\max} < 0.30$, the system recognizes that the signal is transitional or ambiguous and attenuates target torque by 50% toward the rest state, preventing false actuation.
2. **Exponential Moving Average (EMA) Smoothing:** Raw target setpoints are smoothed via an EMA filter ($\alpha = 0.35$):
   $$\theta[t] = \alpha \cdot \theta^{\text{target}}[t] + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \theta[t-1]$$

This mathematical blending ensures that transitions between motor assistance states are smooth, continuous, and completely free of actuator chatter.

## 8. Exoskeleton GUI Architecture and Hardware Integration

To provide a complete operational system for clinical trials, I designed and built a desktop application in Python/Tkinter (`rehab_gui/`), structured across four dedicated pages:

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                               EXOSKELETON REHABILITATION GUI                            |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| [Instructions]  |  [Preliminary Testing]  |  [Run Trial]  |  [Session Analytics]        |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| Page 1: Hardware link setup (USB / Bluetooth, COM port, baud rate, trigger sync)        |
| Page 2: Real-time sensor telemetry, potentiometer calibration, hardware diagnostics     |
| Page 3: Assistance strategy selection, live EMG proportional controller blending        |
| Page 4: Clinical trial session audit, torque curves, gesture activation & CSV export    |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| STATUS BAR: Connection status, battery telemetry, and real-time packet confirmation     |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### 8.1 Functional Page Breakdown
1. **Instructions & Connection (`instructions.py`):** Configures physical communication links (USB vs. Bluetooth), COM ports, baud rates (115200), and lab-system synchronization triggers. Settings are persisted across sessions in `config.json`.
2. **Preliminary Testing (`preliminary_testing.py`):** Runs live dual-axis Matplotlib plots (joint angle, assistive torque, and motor current) at ~30 Hz. Provides interactive potentiometer calibration (locking min/max range) and multi-threaded diagnostic sweeps (torque linearity, joint impedance, and motor speed).
3. **Run Trial (`run_trial.py`):** Allows selecting control strategies (Zero Torque, Assist-as-Needed, Trajectory Tracking, Impedance Control), uploads parameter packets to hardware, runs the live EMG confidence blending engine on held-out test data, and records time-stamped trial logs.
4. **Session Analytics (`analytics.py`):** Displays clinical summary metrics (total cycles, peak torque, mean EMG confidence, gait symmetry), visualizes gesture activation distributions, and exports full clinical CSV reports for patient medical charts.

### 8.2 Wire Protocol and Safety Architecture
Communication with the embedded actuator controller occurs over a structured delimited ASCII protocol:
* **Outgoing Command Packet:** `<controller/state/stance/swing/estim>` (e.g., `<1/1/25/35/0>`)
* **Hardware Acknowledgment:** `OK` or `ERR`
* **Incoming Telemetry Stream:** `angle,torque,current,pot_raw\n`

**Safety Mechanisms:**
* Hardware read/write calls execute in dedicated background daemon threads to prevent UI freezes.
* Joint angles are clamped within calibrated potentiometer bounds.
* Watchdog timeouts detect serial disconnection within 1.0 s, automatically reverting motors to Zero Torque mode.

## 9. Critical Limitations and Future Work

While the implemented pipeline achieves excellent performance, an honest evaluation reveals specific engineering and clinical limitations:

1. **Electrode Technology (Dry vs. Wet):** The UCI dataset was captured using dry stainless-steel electrodes from a commercial Myo armband. While convenient, dry electrodes have higher contact impedance and greater susceptibility to motion artifacts than medical-grade Ag/AgCl wet gel electrodes. Validating the pipeline on wet electrodes in our university laboratory is the next step.
2. **Development PC vs. Target Microcontroller:** All latency benchmarks were recorded on an x86 development workstation. While a cycle time of 1.28 ms gives substantial headroom under 500 ms, running the full pipeline on a resource-limited ARM Cortex-A72 (Raspberry Pi 4) will encounter different cache architectures and memory bus contention. Re-measuring latency on the actual physical Pi board remains a priority.
3. **Calibration Sample Diversity:** Our single-repetition calibration protocol improved mean accuracy from 86.5% to 88.2%, but caused minor decreases in two subjects. Expanding the calibration routine to average two gesture repetitions or applying modern domain adaptation techniques (such as Optimal Transport or CORAL alignment) will make the calibration step universally robust.
